Question 0: The job steps, who does them, where time goes, what a better quote wins

Section 1

The steps, as the sources describe them

No source lays out a full step list for a metals service center. Pieced together from the sources below, the work runs in this order:

  1. Intake. The RFQ arrives by email, PDF, spreadsheet, fax, or phone. A steel bar distributor's inside sales team handles about 200 RFQs and purchase orders a week (eAlliance case study, vendor case study, read through a summarizing fetch tool).
  2. Decode the line. Work out which item the buyer means. At that same distributor, a part has many attributes, so a search returns 20 to 30 candidates and the rep has to judge which is right (same eAlliance source, vendor claim).
  3. Check stock and availability. In stock, in production planning, or a substitute (same eAlliance source).
  4. Price it. Estimators measure parts, track down material prices, and gather estimates for machine run time, cleaning, painting, labor, and shipping (SecturaFAB case study of Brunswick Steel, vendor case study, summarizing fetch tool).
  5. Get approval on exceptions. In distribution generally, the rep checks prices across separate systems, requests an exception, and waits for finance or pricing to approve, often by email (Conga author, Distribution Strategy Group, June 2026, summarizing fetch tool; the author works for Conga, which sells quote software).
  6. Send the quote and chase it. Errors found later send the quote back through approvals (same Conga source).

Who does each step

  • Quoting sits with a few experienced people. Brunswick Steel (a Manitoba service center) says quoting was limited to a select few, and its old process leaned on Excel (SecturaFAB case study above, vendor; the speaker is Dallas McInnes, sales manager, via summarizing tool).
  • Product selection depends on staff knowledge. The Conga author says finding the right SKU in a large catalog often relies on experienced staff's knowledge instead of standard process.
  • Even in machine shops, one estimator is the bottleneck. A Practical Machinist poster who was buying a company wrote: "I need to improve the way we respond to quotes- only have 1 estimator and response time is too slow, I believe it is losing us business." Source: user Neeman, Dec 21, 2020, Practical Machinist thread (read directly).

Where the time goes

  • Large jobs at a steel supplier. Utah Steel (Cedar City, Utah) said a large job took about two days to quote with three people on quotes. Requests for alternative materials or gauges forced a full round of manual changes. After buying SecturaFAB, it reports about two hours. Vendor case study, so these are the vendor's published numbers (Utah Steel case study, summarizing fetch tool).
  • Data work, not selling. Brian Hopkins, COO of the consulting firm Distribution Strategy Group, writes that on teams he built, people spent 75% of their time on data preparation, and in another passage 80% of their time hunting through systems, validating data, and assembling quotes by hand. He says complex quotes sometimes took his team up to eight hours. It is one practitioner's observation, not research (Hopkins, July 2025, summarizing fetch tool).
  • Retyping. A pipe, valve, and fitting vendor (SalesAPE, which sells RFQ software) says sales engineers waste hours retyping customer bill-of-material spreadsheets into the ERP and that spec parsing takes days instead of minutes. Its numbers are illustrative, not measured (SalesAPE, July 2026, vendor, summarizing fetch tool).
  • Machine shops. Paperless Parts (a quoting software seller) says it can take up to two hours to quote one part. This is from a sponsored article in Modern Machine Shop (MMS, Nov 2021, sponsored by Paperless Parts, vendor, summarizing fetch tool).

Sample size not stated on the page I read. A different search snippet attributed a 2020 survey of 400+ buyers, and a later piece says 68% from 370 buyers. The two numbers are not the same survey.

Orders won by speed Prokeep Distributors with faster, more accurate quotes are 35 to 50 percent more likely to win. This is Prokeep's own data. It sells order automation. Search summary only. Prokeep

A counterweight worth knowing. Not every RFQ is worth a fast quote. A search snippet from an MDM piece (What to Fix Before Investing in CRM, MDM) says one rep responds to every RFQ and spends hours on projects with little chance of winning, and that qualifying earlier matters more (search summary only; the page would not load). A machine-shop owner says the same thing in his own words: "the overall objective is still to put in the minimum unpaid work until you actually get the gig." Source: user implmex, Apr 18, 2019, Practical Machinist thread (read directly).

Win-rate benchmarks. I found no metals-specific hit rate. A generic quoting glossary from a software vendor gives 25-40% for established customers and 10-20% for new prospects (Zigaflow glossary, search summary only, vendor, not metals).

Question 1: Pain in practitioners' own words, and buyer annoyances

Section 2

What practitioners actually say (read directly)

All quotes below are from pages I read in a browser.

Quoting fast without over-working it.

  • User DanASM, Apr 2, 2020, Practical Machinist: methods to expedite quoting: "It takes me less than 5 min to get a material quote from 4 major suppliers with competitive pricing and delivery." Also: "I have a few customers who want quotes within 72 hours." And: "You cant waste time quoting every job down to the second."
  • Same user on how he builds a number: after requesting quotes 4-5 times "I have a reliable number to quote my customers from experience." This shows the buyer of metal (a shop) gets quick supplier quotes. The service center is the one answering those four-supplier requests.

Buyers shop around by sending the RFQ to everyone.

  • User MikeHalloran, Sep 12, 2011, Eng-Tips: steel plate: "Either way, just fax a RFQ describing what you want to all the service centers you can find, and pick from the quotes." He adds that "Steel service centers are happy to supply cut rectangles to exact dimensions, but charge a little extra for the cross-cuts and for handling the 'small' pieces." The reader of an RFQ is one of several, and extras like cross-cuts change the price.

Small errors multiply on volume parts.

  • User implmex (a shop owner), Apr 18, 2019, Practical Machinist: "with volume parts a small error has big cumulative consequences." This is a machine shop, but the same math applies to a wrong unit or grade on a large order.

Customers ask for odd things, and the quote has to handle them.

  • User wheelieking71, Oct 10, 2014, Practical Machinist: "I do also get asked A LOT, "how much if I supply material?"" Buyers also ask a supplier to quote against leftover pieces that may not exist.

Software and ERP for service centers (the supplier side, read directly).

  • Paul Parsons, VP of sales and marketing at 4GL (an ERP seller), in Metal Center News, Feb 27, 2026 (Service Center Software): "There are things that happen in metal distribution centers that don't happen in generic distribution companies. Metal service centers are cutting into plate. They have remnants that need to be taken into consideration. All of those things generic software struggles with."
  • Shawne O'Connor, director of Paragon Consulting Services (maker of METALWARE): "Being able to work with multiple units of measures, mixing and matching your units. Those are key factors in managing your inventory." Both are ERP sellers, so they have a reason to say generic software falls short. For our decision, the useful point is that multiple units of measure and remnants are everyday realities for service centers.

Pain stated by vendors and consultants (labeled, read through a summarizing tool)

  • Handoffs. The Conga author says every handoff between teams adds a chance of miscommunication and each manual step adds risk, and that sales pushes for speed and lower prices while finance enforces margin limits, which creates exceptions and slow approvals.
  • Alternates. Utah Steel says requests for alternative materials or gauges force a full round of manual changes. This is the closest thing to a rep-side pain about changes after the first quote.
  • Wrong candidate chosen. eAlliance: 20 to 30 look-alike candidates per part, rep judgment decides, wrong choice means a wrong shipment.
  • Vague requests. A fabricator, Staub Manufacturing, writes: "nothing slows down the quotation process more than having to stop and ask questions." Source: Staub, six things you need for fast accurate fabrication quotes (summarizing tool; Staub is a fabricator and the page doubles as marketing). It lists what RFQs often lack: material type, nominal thickness, grade, special sourcing rules, finish, packaging, quality requirements, volume, and deadline.
  • Questions back to the buyer. This supports the "one-click buyer questions" idea in a general way: stopping to ask is the slow step, so questions that are cheap for the buyer to answer matter. It does not prove buyers dislike being asked.

Buyer annoyances (thin)

Evidence that buyers dislike receiving quotes is weak. What I found:

  • No response to a quote request. A buyer, user silentrunning, Mar 18, 2008, wrote that he requests quotes from new vendors and never hears back, and that lead times for basic raw materials and hardware seem unreasonable (Elsmar Cove thread, summarizing tool). It is from 2008 and about general sourcing.
  • Suppliers' view of buyers. Same thread (summarizing tool): suppliers produce many full quotes and land few, dislike "price fishing" with three-quote requests when the mind is already made up, and note that buyers ask for a firm quote when unsure they can buy soon. This is the supplier side of the same relationship.
  • Speed expectation. The Paperless Parts survey above (vendor survey).
  • What the buyer's RFQ asks for, which is the best evidence of what a quote must contain. Real government RFQs (see the next section) ask for unit and total price, a delivery date estimate, shipping included in the price, and proof paperwork. A USACE RFQ says "DO NOT quote shipping separately" and says offers are judged on price, speed of delivery, and past performance. A buyer who gets a quote that leaves these out has to ask again.
  • Quote validity and price firmness. I could not find buyer complaints about this. Supplier terms pages show metals quotes valid for as little as one business day for volatile metals, and others price at the "prevailing price" at time of shipment (Avalon, search summary only; not a metals service center). Treat as background for the price-benchmark track.

Question 3: Costliest reading errors

Section 3

The evidence is a mix of forum stories and vendor marketing. No source gave a cost per error for a service center.

1. Wrong grade or material variant

  • A stainless tubing mix-up (Evident Scientific, which sells XRF analyzers, so it has a reason to tell this story). A manufacturer ordered SS304 tubing and received SS303, which cracks when welded. The paperwork said 304. The error showed up in non-destructive testing after 1,800 pieces were cut and machined, so those pieces were unusable. The article says incorrect certificates and markings raise the risk at the mill, service center, and fabricator. (Evident: The Case of the Cracked Weld, summarizing tool, vendor.)
  • A 410 versus 410-S shipment. A forum thread (Engineers Edge) describes a brake rotor maker who ordered 410 stainless, got 410-S, found out at the first heat-treat step, and was told the vendor would replace the material but not pay the roughly $7,200 in process cost. A reply argues that once you accept the shipment it is your material. I could not open the page (bot check), so this is search summary only: Engineers Edge thread.
  • Takeaway. When the wrong grade is discovered downstream, the buyer's processing cost can far exceed the material cost, and who pays is a fight. This is the strongest case for "ask, don't guess" on grade.

2. Wrong thickness from a gauge number

  • The same gauge number means different thicknesses by metal. For 16 gauge: steel 0.0598 in, galvanized 0.0635 in, stainless 0.0625 in, aluminum 0.0508 in. Source: Xometry, "The Weird Thing About Gauge" (summarizing tool; Xometry sells manufacturing services). Xometry says the table is not comprehensive.
  • A gauge-to-thickness mismatch can mean a part that is out of tolerance. I found no source that put a dollar cost on a gauge error, so the cost is inferred, not documented.

3. Wrong part picked from look-alike candidates

  • eAlliance (steel bar distributor): wrong selections led to wrong shipments. No numbers. Vendor case study.

4. Missing or late paperwork

  • Navy HY-80 plate RFQ. The buyer requires full traceability, complete physical and chemical mill test reports showing MIL-S-16216 compliance on delivery, DFARS 252.225-7009 specialty-metals compliance, and a DX-A1 rated order. The quote itself must confirm MTR capability and include a certificate of compliance. A quote that misses these is non-responsive. Source: HigherGov listing of RFQ N4215826QE068 (summarizing tool). This is a hidden-requirements example in plain sight: the paperwork is in the RFQ, but a rep who reads only the item line misses it.
  • Certs arriving after the material. On an Elsmar Cove quality forum thread, users say it is common for material to arrive before certs, and certs can get lost when a distributor splits a lot. Summarizing tool: Elsmar thread, page 2.
  • A buyer rejecting material for a missing cover letter. User LittleInch, Dec 11, 2019, on Eng-Tips: ASME material certificate? (read directly), answered a poster whose client wanted a certificate tied to an ASME edition: "I find it difficult to believe someone would "reject" materials on these grounds." That shows buyers do set paperwork conditions that surprise suppliers.
  • What a certificate has to show. User marcolino21576, Jan 24, 2012, on Eng-Tips: standard for material test reports (read directly): a 3.1 certificate "must reflect property of the material you are purchasing." So the type of certificate changes what the supplier must deliver.
  • Cost of a missing MTR. One traceability software vendor page claims a 14-day hold and a $240K recall cost per event, with no source (OxMaint, search summary only). I do not rely on it.

5. Wrong unit

  • I found no documented unit-error story. The MCN piece above (read directly) shows service centers mix units of measure routinely, which is the setup for the error.

What this means for the demo (inference, not from a source)

  • The most damaging errors are the ones that travel: grade and certification gaps that surface only after material has been processed or inspected. Those are the places where "ask, don't guess" saves the most.
  • The gauge and unit errors are common and easy to demonstrate, but no source says they cost the most.

Messy line examples

Section 4

Public solicitations only. I removed the names and contact details of government staff. "Verbatim" means I read the text directly.

A. USAF consumable metals RFQ (verbatim, read directly)

Source: BidNet listing of FA302024Q0014 (Air Force, Gulfport MS, closed 2024-02-01). The government's SAM.gov notice is here.

(80 Each) Steel Plate (Low Carbon), A36 3/8" x 4' x 8' or similar
(40 Each) Steel Plate (Low Carbon), A1011- 11 Gage x 4' x 8' or similar
(100 Each) HR Galvanized Sheet 26 Gage x 4' x 10' or similar
(20 Each) Aluminum Plate, 5052-32 - .063 x 4' x 8' or similar.
(20 Each) STAINLESS Steel 2B T-304 - 16 Gage x 4 x 8 or similar

Also in the notice: "Please include Shipping/Delivery in quote. Quested delivery within 30 days ARO. Forklift on site, need to package all steel in stacks of 10 max, due to lift capability."

Why it is messy:

  • "or similar" on every line.
  • The last line writes "4 x 8" with no units; the lines above use feet.
  • "T-304" and "2B" run together.
  • "Aluminum Plate, 5052-32 - .063" is 0.063 in, which is thin enough to be sheet, not plate (my reading, check with the domain track).
  • "A1011" is the ASTM spec for hot-rolled sheet, but the line calls it "Steel Plate" with an 11 gage thickness.
  • The 16 gage stainless should read as 0.0625 in per the Xometry table above, not 0.060 in.

B. USACE steel RFQ (verbatim, read directly)

Source: BidNet listing, USACE St. Paul District, Pleasant Valley, Iowa, April 2023.

ASTM A-572 G50 1.25" x 96" x 144" 3 EA
ASTM A-36 0.75" x 8" x 20' 10 EA
ASTM A-36 W12 x 79 Beam 20' 3 EA
ASTM A-36 Angle 0.75" x 8" x 6" x 20' 4 EA

Why it is messy: mixed inches and feet on one line, "G50" for Grade 50, the quantity at the end of the line, and "Angle 0.75 x 8 x 6" without saying which leg is which. The notice also says to give "unit pricing for each item," include a delivery date estimate, and ship FOB destination with shipping not quoted separately.

C. State of Iowa RFQ, 2021 (verbatim, read directly from the PDF the fetch tool saved)

Source: State of Iowa RFQ 245, DB22245005, dated 2021-07-16. Each line has a short name and a longer description. They do not always agree.

Line 6   Stainless Tubing 1 x 1 x .05
         Stainless Steel Tubing Type 304, 1" x 1" x .065 wall thickness x 20' Long
Line 8   Tubing 1 x 1 x .065
         SquareTubing A513/A500 Grade B, 1 x 1" x .065 wall thickness x 24' Long
Line 12  Cold Rolled Sheets
         Cold Rolled Sheets CQ/A366, 48" x 96" x 18 Gauge
Line 13  Hot Rolled Sheets
         Hot Rolled Sheets CQ/A569, 48" x 96" x 10 Gauge
Line 14  Stainless Sheets  (filed under "Steel, Mild: Bars, Plates, Rods, Sheets, Strips, Tubes, etc.")
         Stainless Steel Sheets Type 304-2B, 60" x 120" x 14 Gauge
Line 2   HR Angle 1 x 1 x 1/8
         Stainless Steel Angle Type 304, 1" x 1" x 1/8" x 20' Long
Line 11  Tubing 1 1/2 x 1 x .083
         Rectangular Tubing A513/A500 Grade B, 1 1/2" x 1" x .083 wall thickness x 24' Long

Why it is messy, from the text itself:

  • Line 6: the short name says ".05" and the description says ".065". The two disagree.
  • Line 2: the short name says "HR Angle" (hot rolled) and the description says "Stainless Steel Angle Type 304". The short name and description point to different materials.
  • Line 14: stainless sheet filed under the "Steel, Mild" commodity heading.
  • Lines 5, 8, 10, 11: "A513/A500 Grade B" lists two specs with a slash. In my background knowledge, A513 is mechanical tubing and A500 is structural tubing, so the slash hides a real choice. Verify with the domain track.
  • Lines 12, 13: "CQ/A366" and "CQ/A569" cite specs that, as I recall, were later replaced by A1008 and A1011. The Air Force RFQ above uses "A1011". Verify with the domain track.
  • Line 8: "1 x 1" x .065" has an inch mark on the second number only.
  • The state RFQ also says a bid is valid for 90 days.

D. Navy HY-80 plate (read through a summarizing fetch tool)

Source: HigherGov listing of N4215826QE068. The summary reported: "PLATE, ALLOY STEEL, GRADE HY-80", spec MIL-S-16216, 5 ft x 20 ft x 0.625 in (5/8") thick, quantity 1, an NSN, and an equivalent reference "(or equal)". Requirements: full traceability, complete physical and chemical MTRs, DFARS 252.225-7009, priority rating DX-A1. The thickness is given as 5/8 in and 0.625 in in two forms. The paperwork requirements are the point of this one.
